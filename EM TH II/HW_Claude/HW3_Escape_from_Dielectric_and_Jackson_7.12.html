Problem 1 — Escape from a dielectric
A point source of light is embedded near the flat surface of a dielectric of index n. Treat the emitted light as plane waves (rays) going out isotropically. Find the fraction of rays that can refract out into the vacuum above.
ANSWER
f = 12 [ 1 − √1 − 1/n2 ]
Glass (n = 1.5) gives 12.7%. For big n it goes as 1/4n2.
Step 1  —  Which rays escape?
Let θ be the angle a ray makes with the surface normal — this is its angle of incidence. Snell's law gives:
n sin θ = n′ sin r Lec 4, slide 7
Above is vacuum, so n′ = 1:
sin r = n sin θ
But sin r cannot exceed 1. So a refracted ray exists only if
n sin θ ≤ 1,   that is   θ < i0,   where  sin i0 = 1/n
If θ > i0, sin r would have to exceed 1 — impossible. No refracted ray exists, so the wave is totally internally reflected. Lec 4, slide 8
So only rays aimed steeply upward escape. Together they fill the escape cone — a cone of half-angle i0 about the normal. Rays aimed downward never reach the surface.
1
Step 2  —  Solid angle of the escape cone
The source shines the same in every direction. So the fraction of light that escapes is just the fraction of directions that escape.
To name a direction in 3D you need two angles:
θ = polar angle — how far you tilt from the normal (like a slope angle)
φ = azimuthal angle — the rotation about the normal (like a compass bearing)
The picture above is a flat slice — it shows one φ only. Spin that whole picture around the dashed normal line and the triangle sweeps out the real 3D cone. That spin is φ.
2
A small patch of directions has height dθ and width sin θ dφ, so its solid angle is
dΩ = sin θ dθ dφ
Why sin θ? Circles near the top of a sphere are small. At θ = 0 the circle is a point (sin 0 = 0); at θ = 90° it is the widest circle (sin 90° = 1).
Integrate over every direction in the cone: θ from 0 to i0, and φ all the way round from 0 to 2π, since the cone looks the same from every side:
Ωescape = ∫0 2πdφ ∫0 i0sin θ dθ = 2π [−cos θ]0 i0 = 2π(1 − cos i0)
Step 3  —  Convert to a fraction of all directions
All directions together make 4π steradians, so the escaping fraction is
f = Ωescape4π = 2π(1 − cos i0)4π = 1 − cos i02
Step 4  —  Express the answer in terms of n
From Step 1, sin i0 = 1/n. Using sin2 + cos2 = 1:
cos i0 = √1 − sin2i0 = √1 − 1/n2
Substitute into Step 3:
FINAL ANSWER
f = 12 [ 1 − √1 − 1/n2 ]

Problem 2 — Jackson 7.12
The time dependence of electrical disturbances in good conductors is governed by the frequency-dependent conductivity (7.58). Consider longitudinal electric fields in a conductor, using Ohm's law, the continuity equation, and the differential form of Coulomb's law.
(a) Show that the time-Fourier-transformed charge density satisfies [σ(ω) − iωε0]ρ(x,ω) = 0.
(b) Using σ(ω) = σ0/(1 − iωτ) with σ0 = ε0ωp2τ, show that for ωpτ ≫ 1 any initial disturbance oscillates at the plasma frequency and decays with constant λ = 1/2τ.
ANSWER
(a)  [σ(ω) − iωε0] ρ(x,ω) = 0
(b)  ω2 + iω/τ − ωp2 = 0  ⇒  ω ≈ ±ωp − i2τ  ⇒  ρ ∝ e∓iωpt e−t/2τ
— it oscillates at ωp and decays with λ = 1/2τ.

The problem tells us to use the first three.
J = σ(ω) E (Ohm — current follows the field)
∇·J + ∂ρ/∂t = 0 (continuity — charge is conserved)

PART (a)  — show that [ σ(ω) − iωε0 ] ρ(x,ω) = 0
Step 1  —  Fourier transform in time
The continuity equation contains ∂ρ/∂t. Part (b) asks which frequency the charge settles at, and a frequency cannot be read off a differential equation — only off an algebraic one. So the derivative has to go.
Assume every quantity oscillates at a single frequency ω:
f(x,t) = f(x,ω) e−iωt f stands for ρ, J or E
f(x,ω) is the amplitude: it depends on position only, and all of the time dependence sits in e−iωt.
Differentiating in time therefore acts on the exponential alone:
∂ρ∂t = ρ(x,ω) (−iω) e−iωt = −iω ρ since ρ(x,ω)e−iωt is ρ itself
That is, a time derivative is equivalent to multiplication by −iω:  ∂/∂t → −iω
Substituting into the continuity equation, every term carries e−iωt, which is never zero and therefore cancels:
∇·J(x,ω) − iωρ(x,ω) = 0
“Fourier transform in time” is the formal name for this substitution — and it is the phrase the question itself uses when it asks for the time-Fourier-transformed charge density.
5
Step 2  —  Substitute Ohm's law, then Coulomb's law → part (a)
We want an equation in ρ alone. Substitute J = σ(ω)E:
σ(ω) ∇·E − iωρ = 0
Now substitute ∇·E = ρ/ε0:
σ(ω)ε0 ρ − iωρ = 0
Multiply by ε0 and factor out ρ:
FINAL ANSWER — PART (a)
[ σ(ω) − iωε0 ] ρ(x,ω) = 0
Divide by (−iω) and this is [ε0 + iσ/ω]ρ = 0 — his conductor permittivity Lec 7, slide 2 set to zero.

PART (b)  — show it oscillates at ωp and decays with λ = 1/2τ
Step 3  —  Take part (a)'s equation and put in the given σ(ω)
Part (b) says “using the representation σ(ω) = σ0/(1−iωτ)”. Using it where? — in the equation part (a) has just produced. So start by writing that down again:
[ σ(ω) − iωε0 ] ρ(x,ω) = 0 (the answer to part (a))
A real disturbance has ρ ≠ 0, so the bracket itself must vanish:
σ(ω) = iωε0
Substitute the given σ(ω) = σ0/(1−iωτ) and clear the fraction:
σ0 = iωε0(1 − iωτ) = iωε0 + ω2ε0τ
Substitute the given σ0 = ε0ωp2τ, then divide by ε0τ:
ω2 + iωτ − ωp2 = 0
Step 4  —  Solve the quadratic
This is a quadratic in ω. With a = 1, b = i/τ, c = −ωp2, and (i/τ)2 = −1/τ2:
ω = − i2τ ± √ωp2 − 1/4τ2
Step 5  —  Use ωpτ ≫ 1
Since ωpτ ≫ 1, the term 1/4τ2 is negligible beside ωp2, so the square root is just ωp:
ω ≈ ±ωp − i2τ
Since ρ(t) ∝ e−iωt, put this ω back in:
ρ(t) ∝ e∓iωpt · e−t/2τ
The real part of ω gives the oscillation; the imaginary part gives the decay.
FINAL ANSWER — PART (b)
oscillates at  ωp ,   decays with  λ = 12τ
