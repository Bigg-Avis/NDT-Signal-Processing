Example 1 — Faraday Rotation Lec 2 s19, Lec 3 s2–4
For propagation along the z-axis, a medium supports left circular polarization with index nL and right circular polarization with index nR. A plane wave in this medium has E(z = 0, t) = x̂E exp(−iωt). Find the values of z where the wave is linearly polarized along the y-axis.
Flow: split the wave into LCP + RCP → fix both from z = 0 → let each run at its own speed → set Ex = 0.
These are his slide lines, in his order. One correction is marked in Step 4.
Step 1  —  Write it as LCP + RCP
The medium gives the two circular polarizations different indices, so each needs its own k.
E(x,t) = EL e i(kLz − ωt) + ER e i(kRz − ωt) Lec 3, s2
EL = ɛLe+,    ER = ɛRe−
e+ = √22( x̂ + iŷ ),     e− = √22( x̂ − iŷ ) Lec 2, s15
Step 2  —  Put z = 0 into the Step 1 equation
z = 0 is the only place the problem tells us the field, so it is the only place we can pin down ɛL and ɛR. Setting z = 0 above, both exponentials become e−iωt:
E(0,t) = EL e−iωt + ER e−iωt
and the problem states what that equals:
E(0,t) = x̂E e−iωt given in the question
Set the two equal:
EL e−iωt + ER e−iωt = x̂E e−iωt Lec 3, s2
e−iωt is never zero, so cancel it from all three terms (this is the strike-through on his slide), then put in EL = ɛLe+ and ER = ɛRe−:
ɛLe+ + ɛRe− = x̂E

Step 3  —  Match the x̂ and ŷ parts
ɛL√22(x̂ + iŷ)  +  ɛR√22(x̂ − iŷ)  =  x̂E Lec 3, s3
Multiply out and collect the x̂ and ŷ parts:
directionleft sideright side
x̂ √22( ɛL + ɛR ) E
ŷ i √22( ɛL − ɛR ) 0
x̂ and ŷ are independent directions — no amount of x̂ can make a ŷ. So the two rows must hold separately. Same as resolving forces: if the resultant points purely along x, the y-components must sum to zero.
The ŷ row: i and √22are not zero, so the bracket must be:
i √22( ɛL − ɛR ) = 0   ⇒   ɛL = ɛR
The x̂ row, with ɛR replaced by ɛL:
√22( ɛL + ɛL ) = E  ⇒  √22( 2ɛL ) = E  ⇒  √2 ɛL = E
ɛL = E√2 = E√2 × √2√2 = E√22 same number, no root underneath
ɛL = ɛR = √22E
What this means. Equal amounts of left- and right-circular. That is what a linear polarization is: two counter-rotating circles of the same size add up to a straight line. Unequal amounts would give an ellipse.

E(x,t) = √22E e+ e i(kLz−ωt) + √22E e− e i(kRz−ωt) Lec 3, s3
Now put e+ and e− in full, from Step 1:

Ex(x,t) = 12E  ( e i(kLz−ωt) + e i(kRz−ωt) )

Example 2 — Application of Faraday Rotation Lec 3 s5
With tan θ = Ey/Ex, find the rotation angle θ(z) of the polarization direction.  [He wrote “HW 1” here and left it unsolved.]
Given: the same field as Example 1.   Want: θ(z), the angle the polarization line makes with x̂.
Route: take Ex and Ey from Example 1 → pull out the common phase → the ratio becomes a single tangent.
Step 1  —  Both components, from Example 1
Ex is Step 3 of Example 1. For Ey, note e+ carries +i/√2 of ŷ and e− carries −i/√2:
Ex = E2 ( e ikLz + e ikRz ) e−iωt
Ey = iE2 ( e ikLz − e ikRz ) e−iωt
Step 2  —  Pull out the common phase
Split each wavenumber into average ± half-difference:
k = kL + kR2,     Δ = (kL − kR) z2 Δ is an angle, not a wavenumber — the z and the ½ are inside it
kLz = kz + Δ,     kRz = kz − Δ
Same as writing 7 and 3 as 5 + 2 and 5 − 2: mean, plus and minus half the gap. Check: kz + Δ = (kL+kR)z/2 + (kL−kR)z/2 = kLz ✓
Substitute, then split and factor the exponentials:
e ikLz + e ikRz = e i(kz + Δ) + e i(kz − Δ)
= e ikze iΔ + e ikze−iΔ = e ikz ( e iΔ + e−iΔ )
6
Example 2, continued
The bracket is now Euler’s formula. Since e iθ = cos θ + i sin θ and e−iθ = cos θ − i sin θ:
e iθ + e−iθ = 2 cos θ the sines cancel
e iθ − e−iθ = 2i sin θ the cosines cancel
So the two combinations are:
e ikLz + e ikRz = 2 e ikz cos Δ
e ikLz − e ikRz = 2i e ikz sin Δ
Why this helps. Before, Ex and Ey were sums of two exponentials that would not combine. Now both carry the same factor eikz and differ only by cos Δ against sin Δ — so dividing them gives a tangent, which is exactly what tan θ = Ey/Ex needs.

Example 2, continued
Step 3  —  Substitute back
Ex = E e i(kz−ωt) cos Δ
Ey = iE2  (2i e ikz sin Δ) e−iωt = −E e i(kz−ωt) sin Δ i × i = −1
Both components now carry the same phase factor ei(kz−ωt). That is what makes the wave linearly polarized at every z — only the direction of the line turns.

Step 4  —  Form the ratio
tan θ = EyEx = − sin Δcos Δ = −tan Δ   ⇒   θ = −Δ
Finally put Δ back, with k = ωn/c:
ANSWER
θ(z) = − (kL − kR) z2 = ω2c ( nR − nL ) z
The plane of polarization turns steadily with z, at the constant rate dθ/dz = (ω/2c)(nR − nL).

Example 3 — Total Internal Reflection Lec 4 s7
When n > n′, the refraction angle r exceeds the incidence angle i. What happens if sin r > 1?
Given: wave going from dense (n) to less dense (n′).   Want: when the transmitted wave stops existing.
Route: Snell → sin r can exceed 1 → cos r turns imaginary → critical angle.
Step 1  —  Snell’s law
n sin i = n′ sin r from phase matching at the surface Lec 3, s12
sin r = nn′ sin i
Step 2  —  If n > n′, this can exceed 1
n/n′ > 1, so for large enough i the right-hand side passes 1 — and no real angle r has sin r > 1.
cos r = √1 − sin2r   ⇒   cos r is imaginary when sin r > 1
An imaginary cos r means the transmitted wave no longer propagates — it becomes an evanescent wave that decays away from the surface. Lec 4, s8
Step 3  —  The boundary case sin r = 1
The changeover is exactly where sin r = 1, i.e. r = 90°:
nn′ sin i0 = 1
ANSWER
i0 = arcsin n′n   —   if i > i0, total internal reflection
Glass to air (n = 1.5, n′ = 1): i0 = 41.8°.

Example 4 — Optical Fiber Lec 4 s11–12
A fiber has core index n1 and cladding index n0, with n0 < n1 (difference only ~1%). Find the largest angle θmax, measured from the fiber axis, for which a ray stays trapped in the core.
Given: n1 core, n0 cladding.   Want: θmax from the axis.
Route: trapping = TIR at the core wall → but θ is measured from the axis, not the wall normal → convert.
Step 1  —  Trapping means TIR at the wall
The ray must be totally internally reflected every time it hits the core–cladding boundary. From Example 3, with the core as the dense medium:
i > i0,    sin i0 = n0n1 Example 3, with n → n1, n′ → n0
Step 2  —  Convert the wall angle to the axis angle
i is measured from the wall normal, which points across the fiber. θ is measured from the axis, which runs along it. The two directions are perpendicular, so
i = 90° − θ   ⇒   sin i = cos θ this is the only geometry step — read it off the figure
Step 3  —  Rewrite the condition
cos θ > n0n1
cos is decreasing, so a larger cos means a smaller θ. The limit is
ANSWER
θmax = cos−1 n0n1
n0 = 1.45, n1 = 1.465 gives θmax ≈ 8.2° — only rays almost parallel to the axis are guided.

Example 5 — Brewster’s angle Lec 4 s13
Assume μ = μ′. For a p-polarized wave, find the incidence angle at which the reflected wave vanishes.  [He wrote “HW 2” before the last algebra step.]
Given: p-polarization, μ = μ′.   Want: iB with E0″ = 0.
Route: p-polarized reflection coefficient → set its numerator to zero → bring in Z = μc/n and Snell → solve for tan i.
Step 1  —  The p-polarized reflection coefficient
E0″E0 = Z cos i − Z′ cos rZ cos i + Z′ cos r Lec 4, s2–3
Step 2  —  No reflection means the numerator vanishes
E0″ = 0  ⇒  Z cos i = Z′ cos r
Step 3  —  Turn the impedances into indices
Z = √μ/ε = μcn Lec 2, s18
With μ = μ′ the μc cancels in the ratio, leaving
ZZ′ = n′n   ⇒   n′ cos i = n cos r ①
Step 4  —  Bring in Snell’s law
n sin i = n′ sin r ② Lec 3, s12
Two equations, two unknowns (i and r). Eliminate r — this is the part left as HW 2.

Example 5, continued
Step 5  —  Eliminate r
From ①: cos r = (n′/n) cos i.  From ②: sin r = (n/n′) sin i.  Put both into sin2r + cos2r = 1:
n′2n2 cos2i + n2n′2 sin2i = 1
Multiply through by n2n′2:
n′4cos2i + n4sin2i = n2n′2
Divide by cos2i and use 1/cos2i = 1 + tan2i:
n′4 + n4tan2i = n2n′2(1 + tan2i)
Collect the tan2i terms:
tan2i ( n4 − n2n′2 ) = n2n′2 − n′4
tan2i n2(n2 − n′2) = n′2(n2 − n′2) the bracket cancels
tan2i = n′2n2
ANSWER
iB = arctan n′n
Air to glass (n = 1, n′ = 1.5): iB = 56.3°. At this angle the reflected light is purely s-polarized — which is what a polarizing filter exploits.
OPTIONALSubstituting iB back gives iB + r = 90° exactly: at Brewster’s angle the reflected and transmitted rays are perpendicular.

Example 6 — X-ray: almost total external reflection Lec 6 s4–5
At X-ray frequencies the index of essentially all matter is slightly less than 1: n ≈ 1 − δ with δ ~ 10−5. A plane wave in vacuum with wave vector kI reflects from a non-magnetic sample; let q = kR − kI. Show that Rs and Rp both vary as q−4 when the grazing angle is small, but not small enough for total external reflection.
Given: n = 1 − δ, δ ≪ 1; grazing angles α1 (in), α2 (out).   Want: R ∝ q−4.
Route: Snell in grazing angles → small-angle expand to get α12 − α22 = 2δ → Fresnel → swap α1 for q.
Why grazing angles. Here the angles are measured from the surface (α), not from the normal (θ), because X-rays only reflect appreciably when they skim the sample. α = 90° − θ, so sin θ becomes cos α.
Step 1  —  Snell, rewritten in grazing angles
sin θ1 = n sin θ2   ⇒   cos α1 = n cos α2 Lec 3, s12
Step 2  —  Expand for small angles
Both grazing angles are tiny, so use cos α ≈ 1 − α2/2:
1 − α122 = n − n α222
Put n = 1 − δ. In the last term n ≈ 1, since δα2 is negligible against everything else:
1 − α122 = 1 − δ − α222
α12 − α22 = 2δ the key relation — used in Step 5
Total external reflection happens when α12 < 2δ (then α2 has no real solution). The problem says we are above that, so α1 > √2δ.
14
Example 6, continued
Step 3  —  Fresnel for s-polarization, in grazing angles
rs = cos θ1 − n cos θ2cos θ1 + n cos θ2 = sin α1 − n sin α2sin α1 + n sin α2 Lec 4, s4
Step 4  —  Small angles again, and n ≈ 1
Rs = |rs|2 = ( α1 − α2α1 + α2 )2 sin α ≈ α
Step 5  —  Bring in Step 2
Multiply numerator and denominator by (α1 + α2), so the numerator becomes the difference of squares we already know:
Rs = ( α12 − α22(α1 + α2)2 )2 = ( 2δ(α1 + α2)2 )2
Well above the critical angle α2 ≈ α1, so (α1+α2)2 ≈ 4α12:
Rs ≈ ( δ2α12 )2   ∝   α1−4
Step 6  —  Replace α1 by q
q is the change in wave vector on reflection. Incident and reflected have the same length k and are mirror images in the surface, so the difference is perpendicular to the surface with magnitude
q = 2k sin α1 ≈ 2k α1   ⇒   α1 = q2k
ANSWER
Rs ≈ ( δ2α12 )2 = ( 2k2δq2 )2  ∝  q−4
Rp goes the same way: at grazing incidence the s and p formulas coincide.

Example 7 — Plasma Lec 6 s6–7
A plasma is a gas of free electrons: there is no restoring force (ω0 = 0) and no damping (γ = 0). Find ε(ω), define the plasma frequency, and say which waves can pass through.
Given: free electrons, density N, ω0 = 0, γ = 0.   Want: ε(ω)/ε0 and the cut-off.
Route: take the bound-charge result from Lec 5 and switch off the two terms that free electrons do not have.
Step 1  —  Start from the harmonically bound charge model
This is the general dielectric constant already derived in lecture — nothing new is introduced here, we only specialise it:
ε(ω)ε0 = 1 + Ne2ε0m Σj fj ( ωj2 − ω2 − iωγj )−1 Lec 5, s7
Step 2  —  Switch off binding and damping
Free electrons: one species, ω0 = 0 (nothing pulls them back) and γ = 0 (no collisions). The bracket collapses to (−ω2):
εε0 = 1 + Ne2ε0m 1−ω2 = 1 − Ne2ε0mω2
Step 3  —  Name the constant
Everything in front of 1/ω2 is fixed by the material, so give it a name. It has units of frequency squared:
ωp2 = Ne2ε0m the plasma frequency
ANSWER
ε(ω)ε0 = 1 − ωp2ω2,     ωp = √Ne2/ε0m

Example 7, continued
Step 4  —  Read off the physics
If ω < ωp then ε/ε0 < 0, so n = √ε/ε0 is imaginary — the wave cannot propagate and is reflected. Above ωp it passes through.
OPTIONALHis two numbers. Laboratory plasma, N ~ 1018 m−3 → ωp ≈ 6×1010 rad/s — microwaves.  Ionosphere, N ~ 1012 m−3 → ωp ≈ 6×107 rad/s — radio. That is why AM radio bounces off the ionosphere and reaches beyond the horizon, while higher frequencies go straight through to satellites.

Example 8 — Reflection from a good conductor, normal incidence Lec 7 s9
What is the reflection coefficient of a good conductor at normal incidence (ω ≪ γ0)?
Given: insulator n1 above, conductor n2 = nr + i ni below, normal incidence.  Want: R.
Route: Fresnel at i = 0 → take the modulus squared → use nr = ni for a good conductor → expand for nr ≫ n1.
Step 1  —  Fresnel at normal incidence
At i = r = 0 both cosines are 1 and the s and p formulas become the same:
R = | nr + i ni − n1nr + i ni + n1 |2 Lec 4, s2–4
Step 2  —  Write out the modulus
|a + ib|2 = a2 + b2, applied top and bottom:
R = (nr − n1)2 + ni2(nr + n1)2 + ni2
Step 3  —  Use the good-conductor property ni = nr
His slide gives, for a good conductor (σ ≫ εrω):
nr = ni = c √μσ2ω both parts of n are equal in a good conductor Lec 7, s3
Substituting ni = nr and expanding the squares:
R = 2nr2 − 2nrn1 + n122nr2 + 2nrn1 + n12 = 1 − 4nrn12nr2 + 2nrn1 + n12
The second form comes from writing numerator = denominator − 4nrn1. It is the useful form because R is close to 1, and this isolates the small part.
18
Example 8, continued
Step 4  —  A conductor has nr ≫ n1
Of the three terms downstairs, 2nr2 is far the largest:
R ≈ 1 − 4nrn12nr2 = 1 − 2n1nr
Step 5  —  Put nr in terms of σ and ω
n1 is the index of the insulator above, so it carries ε1:
n1 = c √μ1ε1,     nr = c √μ2σ22ω Lec 2 s18 · Lec 7 s3
Divide. The c cancels top and bottom:
2n1nr = 2c √μ1ε1c √μ2σ22ω = 2 √μ1ε1 · 2ω / μ2σ2
Both media are non-magnetic, μ1 = μ2, so they cancel too:
= 2 √2ε1ωσ2
Finally take the 2 inside the root. Since 2 = √4, 2√X = √4 √X = √4X:
= √4 × 2ε1ω/σ2 = √8ε1ωσ2 4 × 2 = 8 — that is where the 8 comes from
ε1 is there because it arrived with n1, not from ε0. The result holds for any insulator above, not just vacuum — checked against the exact Fresnel formula with glass above (n1 = 1.5): 0.99958811 against 0.99958803.
ANSWER
R ≈ 1 − 2n1nr = 1 − √ 8ε1ωσ2
The Hagen–Rubens relation Lec 7, s10. R → 1 as ω → 0 or σ → ∞ — a perfect conductor is a perfect mirror.
OPTIONALCopper (σ = 5.9×107 S/m) at 10 GHz: R = 0.99972 — only 0.03% of the power is absorbed per bounce.

Example 9 — Gaussian modulated oscillation Lec 9 s2–5
A pulse starts as u(x,0) = e−x2/2L2 cos k0x with ∂u/∂t(x,0) = 0, in a medium with dispersion relation ω(k) = ν(1 + a2k2/2). Find u(x,t).
Given: the initial shape, zero initial velocity, and ω(k).   Want: u(x,t).
Route: 7.91 gives A(k) → the integral is Gaussian, so it can be done exactly → put A(k) into 7.90 → read off speed and spreading.
Step 1  —  A(k) from the initial conditions
Because ∂u/∂t(x,0) = 0, the second term of Eq. 7.91 drops out and only the initial shape is left:
A(k) = 1√2π ∫ dx  e−ikx u(x,0) Jackson 7.91 with ∂u/∂t(x,0) = 0 Lec 8, s9
Step 2  —  Split the cosine
A cosine is two exponentials, and each pairs with e−ikx to give a clean Gaussian integral:
cos k0x = 12 ( e ik0x + e−ik0x )
A(k) = 12√2π ∫ dx  e−x2/2L2 [ e−i(k−k0)x + e−i(k+k0)x ]
Step 3  —  Do the Gaussian integral
Standard result — the Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian:
∫ dx  e−x2/2L2 e−iqx = L√2π e−q2L2/2
Apply it twice, with q = k − k0 and q = k + k0:
A(k) = L2 [ e−(L2/2)(k−k0)2 + e−(L2/2)(k+k0)2 ]
Two Gaussian humps, centred at k = +k0 and k = −k0, each of width 1/L. The +k0 hump becomes the right-moving pulse, the −k0 hump the left-moving one.

Example 9, continued
Step 4  —  Put A(k) into 7.90
u(x,t) = 12 1√2π ∫ dk  A(k) e i[kx − ω(k)t] + c.c. Lec 9, s3
With ω(k) = ν(1 + a2k2/2) the exponent is quadratic in k, and A(k) is Gaussian — so the k-integral is Gaussian too and can be done exactly. The result is Jackson 7.98:
ANSWER — Jackson 7.98
u(x,t) = 12Re { exp[ −(x − νa2k0t)2 / 2L2(1 + ia2νt/L2) ](1 + ia2νt/L2)1/2 e i[k0x − ν(1+a2k02/2)t]  + (k0 → −k0) }
Step 5  —  Read the answer
Three things can be read straight off, without redoing any algebra:
1 the pulse centre sits at x = νa2k0t, so it travels at the group velocity
vg = dωdk |k0 = νa2k0 Lec 8, s13
2 the carrier ei[k0x−ω(k0)t] moves at the phase velocity ω(k0)/k0 — a different speed;
3 the width is no longer L but grows with time:
width = √L2 + (a2νt/L)2 Lec 9, s6
A short pulse (small L) spreads fastest, because it needs a wide band of k and the different k travel at different speeds.
