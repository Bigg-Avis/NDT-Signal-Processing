Problem 1 — Fresnel equations for s polarization
A plane wave goes from medium (μ, ε) into medium (μ′, ε′) across the plane z = 0, at angle of incidence i. The electric field is perpendicular to the plane of incidence (s polarization). Find the reflected and transmitted amplitudes — i.e. derive Jackson Eq. (7.39).
ANSWER
Impedance form (Lecture 4), Z = √μ/ε:
E0′E0 = 2Z′cos iZ′cos i + Z cos r ,     E0″E0 = Z′cos i − Z cos rZ′cos i + Z cos r
The same result in n, n′, i — this is Eq. (7.39), the form asked for:
E0′E0 = 2n cos in cos i + μμ′ √n′2−n2sin2i
E0″E0 = n cos i − μμ′ √n′2−n2sin2in cos i + μμ′ √n′2−n2sin2i
Step 0  —  How the professor sets up any interface problem
Lecture 3, slides 6–12 — learn this recipe once; every reflection problem follows it.
1. Write three waves and list what is unknown
incident (known):  E = E0e i(k·x−ωt),   B = √με k×Ek Lec 3, slide 8
reflected:  E″ = E0″e i(k″·x−ω″t)     transmitted:  E′ = E0′e i(k′·x−ω′t) Lec 3, slides 8–9
unknown:  E0′,  E0″,  k′,  k″,  ω′,  ω″ the professor's own list, slide 9
1
2. Kinematics first — this kills the direction unknowns
The boundary conditions hold at every point of z = 0 and every time — only possible if all three phases are equal there.
ω = ω′ = ω″  and   k·x = k′·x = k″·x Lec 3, slide 11
⇒  k sin i = k″sin r′ = k′sin r  ⇒  r′ = i  and   n sin i = n′sin r Lec 3, slide 12
3. Dynamics second — only the amplitudes are left
Two unknowns remain, E0′ and E0″, so we need two equations. They come from Maxwell at the surface:
1 [ ε(E0+E0″) − ε′E0′ ] · n = 0 D⊥ continuous
2 ( E0+E0″ − E0′ ) × n = 0 E∥ continuous
3 [ k×E0 + k″×E0″ − k′×E0′ ] · n = 0 B⊥ continuous
4 [ 1μ(k×E0 + k″×E0″) − 1μ′(k′×E0′) ] × n = 0 H∥ continuous Lec 3, slide 10 = Jackson 7.37
4. Choose a polarization
E can point anywhere perpendicular to k. Split into the two natural cases: E in the plane of incidence (p) — done in class, Lecture 4 slides 2–3 — and E perpendicular to it (s), which is this homework (Lecture 4 slide 4, marked “HW 2”).
STARTING POINT — this is all you carry into Step 1:
•  the four boundary conditions 1–4 above;   •  r′ = i and n sin i = n′sin r;   •  s polarization ⇒ E ⊥ plane of incidence.

Step 1  —  Set up coordinates and write the vectors
Same axes as the professor's picture (Lecture 3, slide 7): interface at z = 0 so n̂ = ẑ, plane of incidence = x–z. Then “E perpendicular to the plane of incidence” means E along ŷ.
E0 = E0y,   E0″ = E0″y,   E0′ = E0′y the two unknowns are E0′, E0″
k = k(sin i x + cos i z),    k′ = k′(sin r x + cos r z)
k″ = k(sin r′ x − cos r′ z) = k(sin i x − cos i z) using r′ = i from Step 0
Where these three lines come from
•  E along ŷ — the plane of incidence is x–z, so “perpendicular to it” can only mean ±ŷ. Allowed for all three waves because k·E = 0 and all three k lie in the x–z plane.
•  k and k′ — a direction at angle θ measured from the normal ẑ is sin θ x̂ + cos θ ẑ. Multiply by the magnitudes |k| = ω√(με), |k′| = ω√(μ′ε′). Lec 3, slide 9
•  k″ — minus on ẑ because the reflected wave goes back down; magnitude is k, not k″, since |k″| = ω″√(με) = ω√(με) = k. Lec 3, slide 9

Step 2  —  Which of the four boundary conditions are useful?
The professor ticks the two that work and moves on (Lecture 4, slide 2). For s polarization they turn out to be the same two he used for p: ② and ④.
Reminder — the four conditions from p. 2 (Lec 3, slide 10):
1 D⊥ continuous (normal D)    2 E∥ continuous (tangential E)
3 B⊥ continuous (normal B)    4 H∥ continuous (tangential H)
1 D⊥: E is along ŷ, which is tangential, so D⊥ = 0 on both sides ⇒ 0 = 0, nothing.
3 B⊥: gives k sin i (E0+E0″) = k′sin r E0′, and Snell (k sin i = k′sin r) lets you divide that common factor off both sides, leaving E0+E0″ = E0′ — the same equation as ② ⇒ nothing new.
✓ 2 and ✓ 4 are the two that give the answer.
Step 3  —  Write out 2 and 4
Read the tangential components straight off the figure, using |H| = |E|/Z from the impedance relation (Lecture 2).
2 ⇒  E∥ + E∥″ = E∥′ E is along ŷ, i.e. entirely tangential → no cos
E0 + E0″ = E0′   …(I)
4 ⇒  H∥ + H∥″ = H∥′ H lies in the plane → tangential part = H cos
E0Zcos i − E0″Zcos i = E0′Z′cos r   …(II)
The minus sign: H ∝ k̂ × E, and the incident and reflected waves travel opposite ways in z, so their tangential (x̂) parts point opposite ways — exactly the same minus that appears in the professor's p‑polarization equation E0cos i − E0″cos i = E0′cos r.
Compare with his p case: there H was ⊥ the plane (no cos) and E was in it (with cos). For s the two swap over. (I) and (II) are Jackson's Eq. (7.38).
4
Step 4  —  Solve the two equations
Use (I) to remove E0″, then read off E0′.
From (I): E0″ = E0′ − E0. Put into (II):
cos iZ ( 2E0 − E0′ ) = cos rZ′ E0′  ⇒  2E0cos iZ = E0′ Z′cos i + Z cos rZ Z′
STEP 4 — halfway, not Eq. (7.39) yet
E0′E0 = 2Z′cos iZ′cos i + Z cos r ,    E0″E0 = E0′E0 − 1 = Z′cos i − Z cos rZ′cos i + Z cos r
This matches the professor's Lecture 4 slide exactly — but it still contains Z, Z′ and cos r. Eq. (7.39) uses n, n′ and i only, so one more step is needed.
Step 5  —  Rewrite it the way Jackson does
Two substitutions: replace Z by n, then remove r using Snell.
(a)  n = c√με ⇒  Z = √μ/ε = μ√με = μcn ,   Z′ = μ′cn′
Both come straight off one slide Lec 2, plane waves in a nonconducting medium: vp = 1/√(με) and n = c/vp give √(με) = n/c, and Z = √(μ/ε). The one trick is to multiply inside the root by μ:  √(μ/ε) = √(μ2/με) = μ/√(με) = μ/(n/c) = μc/n.
E0′E0 = 2(μ′c/n′)cos i(μ′c/n′)cos i + (μc/n)cos r = 2n cos in cos i + (μ/μ′) n′cos r × nn′/(μ′c)
(b)  Snell n sin i = n′sin r  ⇒  n′cos r = √n′2 − n2sin2i
FINAL ANSWER = JACKSON (7.39)
E0′E0 = 2n cos in cos i + μμ′ √n′2−n2sin2i
E0″E0 = n cos i − μμ′ √n′2−n2sin2in cos i + μμ′ √n′2−n2sin2i

Problem 2 — Brewster's angle
Show that for p polarization (E parallel to the plane of incidence) there is an angle at which nothing is reflected, and find it. Take μ = μ′. — Jackson Eq. (7.43).
ANSWER
iB = tan−1 n′n
Only p polarization has such an angle. Air → glass: 56.3°; glass → air: 33.7°.
Step 0  —  Where does this start?
You do not redo Problem 1. The professor already derived the p-polarization coefficient in class by exactly the same four-boundary-condition route; you start from his result.
E0″E0 = Z cos i − Z′cos rZ cos i + Z′cos r Lec 4, slides 3 & 13 Z and Z′ swapped vs. s
Compare with the s result on p. 4: the only difference is which impedance sits with cos i. That swap is the entire reason Brewster's angle exists for p and not for s.
Step 1  —  When is the reflected amplitude zero?
A fraction is zero when its numerator is zero.
E0″ = 0  ⇔   Z cos i = Z′cos r
Step 2  —  Write it in terms of n
Put Z = μc/n (same bridge as Problem 1, Step 5). With μ = μ′ the μc cancels.
μcncos i = μcn′cos r   ⇒   n′cos i = n cos r  …(A)
and Snell always holds:  n sin i = n′sin r  …(B)
6
Step 3  —  Combine (A) and (B)
Multiply them together — the n's cancel and a double angle appears.
n n′ sin i cos i = n n′ sin r cos r  ⇒  sin i cos i = sin r cos r  ⇒  sin 2i = sin 2r
Two possibilities: 2i = 2r (means n = n′, no interface — reject), or 2i = 180° − 2r:
iB + r = 90°
Step 4  —  Put that back into Snell
n sin iB = n′sin(90° − iB) = n′cos iB   ⇒   tan iB = n′/n
FINAL ANSWER = JACKSON (7.43)
iB = tan−1 n′n    (equivalently  iB + r = 90°)
■  END OF REQUIRED SOLUTION — everything below is optional  ■
OPTIONALSame answer without the double-angle trick. From (A) cos r = (n′/n)cos i and from (B) sin r = (n/n′)sin i. Then sin2r + cos2r = 1 gives (n2/n′2)sin2i + (n′2/n2)cos2i = 1, hence sin2iB = n′2/(n2+n′2) and cos2iB = n2/(n2+n′2), so tan iB = n′/n. ✓

EXTRA — none of this page is required for the answer
Why s polarization has no Brewster angle
From Problem 1, E0″ = 0 would need Z′cos i = Z cos r, i.e. (with μ = μ′)  n cos i = n′cos r.
Combine with Snell n sin i = n′sin r — square both and add: n2 = n′2 — only if there is no interface at all.
So rs never vanishes, at any angle. Light reflected at iB is therefore 100% s polarized — that is how polarizing sunglasses and camera filters remove glare.
Physical picture
iB + r = 90° means the reflected direction is perpendicular to the refracted one. For p polarization the transmitted E′ is perpendicular to k′, so it points exactly along the reflected direction — and a dipole radiates nothing along its own axis.
