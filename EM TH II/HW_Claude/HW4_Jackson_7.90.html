Problem — General solution of the 1D wave equation
Derive Jackson Eq. 7.90, the general solution of the one-dimensional wave equation.
What to write: Steps 0 → 3, ending at Eq. 7.90. That is the whole answer. Everything after the dashed line on page 4 is background, not required.
ANSWER
u(x,t) = 12 1√2π ∫−∞∞ dk  A(k) e i[kx − ω(k)t]  +  c.c.
c.c. = complex conjugate of the term before it. A(k) is a free function — that is what makes the solution general.

Step 0  —  Starting point
Maxwell in vacuum gives the wave equation:
∇2E − μ0ε0 ∂2E∂t2 = 0,    μ0ε0 = 1c2 Lec 1, slide 13
Each component obeys it separately. Take Ex, expand in a Fourier integral:
Ex(x,t) = 1(2π)3/2 ∫ d3k  e ik·x  ɛx(k,t) Lec 1, slide 16
3D → 1D: d3k → dk, (2π)3/2 → √2π. ɛx still depends on t — it is a(k,t) of Step 1, not Jackson’s A(k). Result also stated in Lec 8, slide 9
1
Step 1  —  One dimension; time dependence of each k
Write u = Ex. Jackson uses the name u because every component obeys the same scalar equation — the derivation does not care which field it is.
In 1D, ∇2 = ∂2/∂x2:
∂2u∂x2 − 1c2 ∂2u∂t2 = 0
Write u as a Fourier integral:
u(x,t) = 1√2π ∫ dk  e ikx a(k,t)
Here a(k,t) = how much of the wave eikx is present in u at time t.
Differentiate twice in x — only eikx depends on x, and each ∂/∂x brings down ik:
∂2u∂x2 = 1√2π∫ dk (ik)2e ikxa = 1√2π∫ dk (−k2) e ikxa
Differentiate twice in t — only a depends on t:
∂2u∂t2 = 1√2π∫ dk e ikx ∂2a∂t2
Put both into the wave equation:
1√2π∫ dk e ikx [ −k2a − 1c2 ∂2a∂t2 ] = 0
This must hold for every x. A Fourier integral can only vanish for all x if its amplitude vanishes for each k separately — there is nothing left to cancel against. So the bracket is zero; multiplying it by −c2:
∂2a∂t2 + k2c2 a = 0 (Helmholtz) Lec 1, slide 18
What this achieved. u(x,t) depended on two variables and obeyed a partial differential equation. a(k,t) depends on t only — k is just a label — so it obeys an ordinary differential equation, one for each k. That is the whole point of the Fourier integral.
2
Step 1, continued
Its two solutions are e−iωt and e+iωt:
a(k,t) = C(k) e−iωt + D(k) e+iωt,     ω = c|k| Lec 1, slide 18
C and D are the two constants of integration, fixed in Step 3.
Slide 18 writes ω = kc, where k = |k| is a 3D magnitude, never negative. In 1D k is signed, so ω = c|k| — even in k, as needed next.
ω = c|k| is the vacuum answer. In glass or water the speed depends on frequency, so the curve is different. Jackson therefore writes ω = ω(k), meaning “whatever function of k it happens to be”, so that 7.90 holds in any medium:
ω = ω(k),   ω(−k) = ω(k) Jackson 7.79
The second condition says a wave going left has the same frequency as the same wave going right. It is used in Step 3, where k is flipped to −k.
3
Step 2  —  Superposition over all k
The method in three moves: Step 1 first broke u into one piece per k, then solved each piece. Step 2 glues the pieces back together.
Put a(k,t) of Step 1 back into the Fourier integral of Step 1:
u(x,t) = 1√2π ∫ dk  e ikx [ C(k) e−iωt + D(k) e+iωt ]
Combine the exponentials in each term, e ikxe ∓iωt = e i(kx ∓ ωt):
u(x,t) = 1√2π ∫−∞∞ dk  [ C(k) e i(kx − ωt) + D(k) e i(kx + ωt) ]
Both branches are kept. Nothing is discarded here — Step 3 will show that D is not free, it is fixed by C.
This is the general solution because:
1 the equation is linear → any sum of solutions is a solution;
2 C(k) and D(k) are free for every k.
One k = an endless sine wave. A band of k added together = a pulse:

Step 3  —  Make u real  →  Eq. 7.90
u is a physical field, so it must be real:  u = u*.
Conjugate the whole expression:
u* = 1√2π ∫ dk  e−ikx [ C*(k) e+iωt + D*(k) e−iωt ]
Why relabel. Compare the two lines: u carries e +ikx, but u* carries e−ikx. Different x-dependence, so the coefficients cannot be matched yet. Substituting k → −k in the u* integral flips it:
beforeafter k → −k
the x-factore−ikx e +ikx ✓
the amplitudeC*(k) C*(−k)
the frequencyω(k) ω(−k) = ω(k)
the limits−∞ → ∞ ∞ → −∞, and dk → −dk
The last row: the flipped limits and the minus on dk cancel each other, so the integral is unchanged. That works only because the range is symmetric. k is a dummy variable — like the i in Σi — so renaming it changes nothing: ∫−∞∞f(k) dk = ∫−∞∞f(−k) dk.
This is the one place ω(−k) = ω(k) is used. Without it the exponents would not match either, and the comparison would fail.
So:
u* = 1√2π ∫ dk  e ikx [ C*(−k) e+iωt + D*(−k) e−iωt ]
Now set u = u*. Both are ∫dk eikx[…], so match the two time-pieces separately:
piecefrom ufrom u*gives
e−iωtC(k) D*(−k) C(k) = D*(−k)
e+iωtD(k) C*(−k) D(k) = C*(−k)
The two rows say the same thing. This is the same move as matching the x̂ and ŷ parts separately — two independent pieces, so each must balance on its own. Note the −k: D is the conjugate of C at the opposite wavenumber.
5
Step 3, continued
So D is not thrown away — it is forced. Put it back:
u(x,t) = 1√2π ∫ dk  e ikx [ C(k) e−iωt + C*(−k) e+iωt ]
Relabel k → −k in the second piece only — it turns into the conjugate of the first piece:
u(x,t) = 1√2π ∫ dk  C(k) e i(kx − ωt)  +  c.c.
Finally write A(k) = 2C(k), which is Jackson’s amplitude:
FINAL ANSWER — Jackson 7.90
u(x,t) = 121√2π ∫−∞∞ dk  A(k) e i[kx − ω(k)t]  +  c.c.
The “+ c.c.” is the D-branch, rewritten. A(k) stays a free function — that is what makes the solution general.

1. What A(k) is, if you are given a starting wave (Jackson 7.91).
2nd order in t needs two conditions: u(x,0) and ∂u/∂t(x,0). Put t = 0 in 7.90, then differentiate 7.90 and put t = 0:
u(x,0) = 121√2π ∫ dk  [ A(k) + A*(−k) ] e ikx
∂u∂t(x,0) = 121√2π ∫ dk  (−iω)[ A(k) − A*(−k) ] e ikx
Invert both, then add them — A*(−k) cancels:
A(k) = 1√2π ∫ dx  e−ikx [ u(x,0) + iω(k) ∂u∂t(x,0) ]
