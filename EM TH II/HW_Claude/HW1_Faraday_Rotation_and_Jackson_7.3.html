Problem 1 — Faraday rotation: θ(z)
In the Faraday-rotation example the medium has index nL for left-circular and nR for right-circular polarization. At z = 0 the wave is linearly polarized along x̂, i.e. E(z=0, t) = x̂ E e−iωt. Using tan θ = Ey/Ex (both real), find how fast the plane of polarization rotates with z.
ANSWER
θ(z) = − (kL − kR) z2 = ω2c ( nR − nL ) z
dθdz = ω2c ( nR − nL )   =  a constant  [rad/m]
θ grows linearly in z, so the rotation rate never changes along the path. The wave stays linearly polarized everywhere — only its direction turns.
Step 0  —  Where does the starting equation come from?
This is the part you have to know before you can write anything. It is all on Lecture 3, slides 2–3.
Why write the wave as two circular waves?
Because the problem hands you two indices, nL and nR. An index belongs to a wave that travels without changing shape. So in this medium the only two waves that propagate cleanly are left- and right-circular, each with its own k = ωn/c. A linearly polarized wave is not a natural wave here — it has to be built out of those two.
E(z,t) = EL e i(kLz−ωt) + ER e i(kRz−ωt) Lec 3, slide 2
EL = ℰL e+,   ER = ℰR e−,   e± = 1√2 (x ± i y) Lec 2, complex unit vectors
ℰL and ℰR are two unknown numbers. Everything else above is known. Finding them is the whole of Step 0.
1
How do we fix ℰL and ℰR?
Use the one piece of information the problem gives: what the wave looks like at z = 0. Set z = 0 in the equation above; the two exponentials both become e−iωt and cancel against the right-hand side.
E(0,t) = ( ℰLe+ + ℰRe− ) e−iωt = x E e−iωt given
⇒   ℰL√2 (x + iy) + ℰR√2 (x − iy) = x E
compare y:  i√2 ( ℰL − ℰR ) = 0  ⇒  ℰL = ℰR
compare x:  1√2 ( ℰL + ℰR ) = E  ⇒  ℰL = ℰR = E√2
STARTING EQUATION — everything from here on is worked out from this one line:
E(z,t) = E√2 e+ e i(kLz−ωt) + E√2 e− e i(kRz−ωt)
= the last line of Lecture 3, slide 3
Step 1  —  Split it into x and y components
Put e± = (x̂ ± iŷ)/√2 into the starting equation and collect the x̂ terms and the ŷ terms.
Ex = E√2 · 1√2 ( e ikLz + e ikRz ) e−iωt = E2 ( e ikLz + e ikRz ) e−iωt
Ey = E2 ( i e ikLz − i e ikRz ) e−iωt = i E2 ( e ikLz − e ikRz ) e−iωt
2
Step 2  —  Combine the two exponentials
Standard trick: pull out the average phase, and what is left is a cos and a sin.
Let  k = kL+kR2  (average),   Δk = kL − kR  (difference).  Then kL = k + Δk/2 and kR = k − Δk/2, so
e ikLz ± e ikRz = e ikz ( e iΔkz/2 ± e−iΔkz/2 ) = 2 e ikz cosΔkz2  or  2i e ikz sinΔkz2
⇒   Ex = E e i(kz−ωt) cos Δkz2 ,     Ey = −E e i(kz−ωt) sin Δkz2
(Ey: the i from Step 1 times the 2i from the sine gives i·i = −1.) Both components carry the same factor E ei(kz−ωt); divide it out and only cos and −sin remain, which are real. That is the “Ex, Ey both real” the problem mentions.
Step 3  —  Take the ratio and read off θ
The common factor cancels in the ratio, which is why θ is a real angle.
tan θ(z) = EyEx = −sin(Δkz/2)cos(Δkz/2) = tan( − Δkz2 )   ⇒   θ(z) = − Δkz2
with k = ωnc Lec 2 :   Δk = ωc (nL−nR)
FINAL ANSWER
θ(z) = ω2c (nR−nL) z      dθdz = ω2c (nR−nL) = πλ0 (nR−nL)
■  END OF REQUIRED SOLUTION — everything below is optional  ■
Over a cell of length d the plane has turned by β = (ω/2c)(nR−nL)d — the angle β on the “Application of Faraday Rotation” slide. In a magnetized medium (nL−nR) ∝ B, giving θ = V B d with V the Verdet constant.

Problem 2 — Jackson 7.3
7.3  Two semi-infinite slabs of the same dielectric (index n, nonpermeable, lossless) are parallel, separated by an air gap (n = 1) of width d. A plane wave of frequency ω hits the gap from inside one slab at angle of incidence i. For polarization both parallel to and perpendicular to the plane of incidence: (a) find transmitted/incident power and reflected/incident power; (b) for i above the critical angle, sketch transmitted/incident power vs. d in units of the wavelength in the gap.
ANSWER
cos r = √1−n2sin2i (angle inside the gap),  β = ωdccos r (phase for one crossing of the gap).
(a)  both polarizations:
T = 11 + F sin2β ,   R = 1 − T,   Fs = (n2−1)24n2cos2i cos2r ,   Fp = Fs[(n2+1)sin2i − 1]2
(b)  above critical angle: γ = √n2sin2i−1, α = 2πγd/λ, and sin2β → −sinh2α:
Ts = [ 1 + (n2−1)24n2γ2cos2i sinh2α ]−1,   Tp = same × [(n2+1)sin2i−1]2
Step 0  —  How do you even start this one?
Nothing new is needed — it is the lecture's single-interface problem used twice, plus bookkeeping for the bounces in between.
Recognise the shape of the problem
1.  Two interfaces (slab→gap at z = 0, gap→slab at z = d).
2.  At each one, the lecture already gives r and t Lec 4, slides 3–4.
3.  Inside the gap the wave bounces back and forth forever ⇒ add up a geometric series.
4.  Above the critical angle the gap wave is evanescent Lec 4, slide 8, so cos r goes imaginary — that is all part (b) is.
So the plan is: angles → one-interface coefficients → sum the bounces → square it. Steps 1–4 below are exactly those four things.
4
Step 1  —  Angles: Snell twice
Same kinematics as Lecture 3 — do the angles before touching amplitudes.
slab → gap:  n sin i = 1·sin r  ⇒  sin r = n sin i,   cos r = √1 − n2sin2i Lec 3, slide 12
gap → slab:  1·sin r = n sin i′  ⇒  i′ = i exit angle = entry angle
critical angle:  sin ic = 1/n
Slab 3 is the same medium as slab 1 and the exit angle is the same, so the power ratio is just T = |t|2 — the flux factor (Z/Z′)(cos r/cos i) from Lecture 4 equals 1 here.
Step 2  —  Reflection at one interface
Straight from the lecture's Fresnel equations, written for slab (n) → air (1), i.e. Z = Z0/n and Z′ = Z0.
rs = Z′cos i − Z cos rZ′cos i + Z cos r = n cos i − cos rn cos i + cos r ,    rp = Z cos i − Z′cos rZ cos i + Z′cos r = cos i − n cos rcos i + n cos r Lec 4, sl. 3–4
Going the other way (air → slab) swaps the two media in those same formulas:
r21 = −r12  and   t12t21 = 1 − r122 swap n ↔ 1 and i ↔ r to check
Write r for r12 from here on. Everything below is identical for s and p — only the value of r differs.
Step 3  —  Add up every bounce inside the gap
Each crossing of the gap multiplies the amplitude by eiβ, where β is the phase gained by the z-component of k:  β = (ωd/c)cos r.

⇒  T = |ttot|2 = (1−r2)2(1−r2)2+4r2sin2β = 11 + F sin2β ,   F ≡ 4r2(1−r2)2

Only F changes between the two polarizations.
1 − rs2 = (n cos i+cos r)2 − (n cos i−cos r)2(n cos i+cos r)2 = 4n cos i cos r(n cos i+cos r)2

same for p:  cos2i − n2cos2r = (n2−1)[(n2+1)sin2i − 1]

FINAL ANSWER — PART (a)
Fs = (n2−1)24n2cos2i cos2r ,    Fp = (n2−1)2[(n2+1)sin2i−1]24n2cos2i cos2r
Ts,p = [ 1 + Fs,p sin2β ]−1,    Rs,p = 1 − Ts,p,    β = (ωd/c) cos r
Step 6  —  Above the critical angle → part (b)
Now sin r = n sin i > 1, so cos r cannot be a real number — it goes imaginary. This is the evanescent wave of Lecture 4.
cos r = i γ,  γ = √n2sin2i − 1 Lec 4, slide 8  ⇒  β = i α,  α = ωdγc = 2πγ dλ
sin2β = sin2(iα) = −sinh2α   and   cos2r = −γ2
the two minus signs cancel inside F sin2β, so T stays real and positive.
FINAL ANSWER — PART (b)
Ts = [ 1 + (n2−1)24n2γ2cos2i sinh2α ]−1 ,   Tp = [ 1 + (n2−1)2[(n2+1)sin2i−1]24n2γ2cos2i sinh2α ]−1
The gap is vacuum so its wavelength is λ, and d/λ is exactly the axis Jackson wants. sinh is monotonic ⇒ T falls smoothly from 1 to 0, no oscillation. For d ≫ λ: T ∝ e−4πγd/λ (photon tunnelling).
